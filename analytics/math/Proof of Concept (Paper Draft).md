## §2.3 Proof of Concept

Fix a learning rule (here we consider Roth–Erev for conceptual clarity, but most of the observations hold for the others), the choice rule, and a pair of matching games — one per agent. At any moment, the system has a joint policy: each agent's signaling table $f^{(i)}$ (which signal to emit given an observation) and its action table $g^{(i)}$ (which action to take given an observation and the partner's incoming signal). I write $f^{(i)}[\mathbf{o}_i]$ for the signal agent $i$ emits given observation $\mathbf{o}_i$, and $g^{(i)}[(\mathbf{o}_i, s)]$ for the action it takes given its own observation $\mathbf{o}_i$ and received signal $s$. Each is, strictly, a sample from the corresponding row of the table; nothing in §2.3 turns on the distinction between the row (a distribution over signals or actions) and the realized value (the emitted signal or chosen action), so I let context fix which is meant. With two agents, I write $j$ for the partner of agent $i$. A single episode then unfolds as follows. Nature draws a world state $\mathbf{v}$; each agent $i$ emits signal $f^{(i)}[\mathbf{o}_i]$ and, having received the partner's signal $f^{(j)}[\mathbf{o}_j]$, takes action $g^{(i)}[(\mathbf{o}_i,\, f^{(j)}[\mathbf{o}_j])]$; each agent collects reward $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ from its own matching game; and finally a learning update adjusts the cells of $f^{(i)}$ and $g^{(i)}$ that were just used.

The crucial observation is that, with the learning rule and payoffs fixed, all of an episode's randomness is generated by Nature's initial sampling. We can therefore take the *state* of the system, in the Markov-chain sense, to be the joint policy $\sigma_t = (f^{(0)}_t, g^{(0)}_t, f^{(1)}_t, g^{(1)}_t)$, together with whatever bookkeeping the choice rule requires. The per-episode draws are *outputs* of the transition, not part of the state. This presents the signal-trading game as a Markov chain on the space of joint policies, and reduces "do the agents learn to communicate?" to a question about which regions of that space the chain enters and where it concentrates over time.

Call a joint policy *ideal* if both agents earn reward $1$ on every world state. Under Roth–Erev, two conditions must hold simultaneously: each $f^{(i)}$ must be a bijection from observation to signal, so that the signal carries the partner's observation losslessly; and each $g^{(i)}$ must be the unique action map satisfying $g^{(i)}[(\mathbf{o}_i,\, f^{(j)}[\mathbf{o}_j])] = \alpha_i^\star(\mathbf{v})$ for every $\mathbf{v}$. In the canonical 2-feature, 2-signal, 4-action setup considered here, there are exactly four such ideal joint profiles. The proof of concept asks whether the dynamics carries the chain into one of those four — or close enough to one — despite the fact that each agent's reward is independent of the partner's action.

It is sometimes said that the ideal strategies are *attractors*: the closer the policy is to one, the more likely the next step gets even closer. This is true *locally* but not *globally*, and the distinction matters. *Locally*: once the chain is near an ideal $\sigma^\star$, ideal profiles collect reward $1$ by definition, so the cells whose reinforcement moves the policy toward $\sigma^\star$ get reinforced more often than the cells whose reinforcement moves it away. The local pull is genuine. *Globally*: that local pull says nothing about whether the chain enters the basin of $\sigma^\star$ from a generic starting point in the first place. Whether it does is the question §2.3 addresses, by varying initial conditions and reading the outcome off simulation.

### The Figure

In [**Figure 1.**](../../results/proof_of_concept/proof_of_concept_plot_RE.png) the initial state of an agent's signaling table is controlled by a single integer parameter $n$; we initialize each row as a one-hot vector with weight $n$ on a randomly chosen "hot" cell and weight $1$ on every other "cold" cell. So agent $i$'s table encodes an approximate (random) bijection from observation to signal, and agent $j$'s table encodes another bijection sampled independently. Action tables are always initialized uniformly, so the only knob that varies is *how strongly each agent's initial signaling table commits to an arbitrary, and not necessarily shared, bijection*. At $n = 1$ both rows are uniform: there is no initial commitment at all, and both agents start at maximum entropy on signal emission. At $n = 100$ each row is sharply peaked at its hot cell — on the first episode an agent emits the hot signal for its observed feature with probability $n / (n + 1) \approx 99\%$ — and the question we ask here is whether the chain finds its way into the basin of an ideal joint policy from initial conditions of varying strength, including the limiting case where the two agents' independently sampled bijections happen to be incompatible.

The figure shows the final reward (left panel) and final normalized mutual information between the emitted signal and the agent's own observation (right panel), plotted against initial signaling bias $n$ (log scale, $n \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 25, 50, 100\}$). Within each panel, the seven curves correspond to a horizon ladder $H \in \{30, 50, 100, 300, 1{,}000, 3{,}000, 10{,}000\}$ — the number of episodes the chain has been allowed to run — coloured from dark (short) to bright (long). The "final" value at horizon $H$ is the average over the last $\max(10,\ H/10)$ episodes of that window. Solid lines are means across $500$ independent seeds per $(n,\, H)$ pair; shaded bands are mean $\pm$ one standard deviation, clipped to $[0, 1]$. The dotted horizontal line at $0.5$ is the no-signaling reward baseline; the average reward a Bayes-optimal agent would collect if it had to act on its private observation alone, with the partner's signal carrying no information.

### Three observations

*First*, reward rises monotonically along *both* axes of the figure: in initial bias $n$ at fixed horizon, and in horizon $H$ at fixed bias. NMI also rises monotonically in $n$ at fixed $H$, but its dependence on horizon is much weaker; at low bias the NMI curves lie nearly on top of each other across the horizon ladder and only at high bias does horizon marginally separate them. The basin-reachability claim is therefore most visible in the reward panel: *more prior coordination, or more elapsed episodes, brings the chain closer to high reward on average.* The NMI panel adds a complementary point — signaling structure does not develop spontaneously from a uniform start, but it does sharpen with time once the urn has been pre-biased. At $n = 1,\ H = 10{,}000$ the average reward is $\approx 0.65$ and the average NMI is $\approx 0.18$; at $n = 100,\ H = 10{,}000$ both are essentially saturated, at $\approx 0.99$ and $\approx 0.92$. The slope along the bias axis is steepest in the middle of the sweep (roughly $n = 3$ to $n = 25$) and flattens at both ends; the slope along the horizon axis, for the reward panel, is steepest between $H = 100$ and $H = 1{,}000$. Neither axis admits a sharp threshold; both produce smooth, quantitative dependence.

*Second*, the dissociation between *informative* signaling (measured by NMI) and *useful* signaling (measured by reward) is sharpest at intermediate horizons. At $H = 10{,}000$ the two metrics track each other reasonably closely across the bias sweep — high bias drives both into saturation, low bias holds both near floor. At $H = 30$ neither curve has moved appreciably off its initial state, so there is barely any signaling to be informative or useful. The dissociation lives in the transient: at $H = 300,\ n = 100$, average reward sits around $\approx 0.85$ while NMI is around $\approx 0.65$. The receiver decodes well enough to act on the partner's signal while the signaling channel itself is still gaining structure. *Useful* signaling outpaces *informative* signaling in finite time, and the gap is a visible feature of the figure rather than a stipulated framing. The reverse direction — high-NMI signaling that is misaligned across the pair and so fails to produce high reward — is taken up in §3.

*Third*, the chain takes thousands of episodes to inhabit the basin even at the strongest initial bias. At $n = 100,\ H = 100$ — already a strongly biased start — average reward is around $\approx 0.65$; at $H = 1{,}000$ it reaches $\approx 0.93$; only at $H = 3{,}000$ and beyond does it saturate. At low bias the timescale is longer still: at $n = 1$ the reward curve climbs from $\approx 0.30$ at $H = 30$ to $\approx 0.65$ at $H = 10{,}000$, and the asymptote is approached but not reached. This is the quantitative content of "in finite time": basin reach is not an asymptotic statement deferred to $t \to \infty$ but a measurable timescale of thousands of episodes that depends on initialization and on the additive structure of the Roth–Erev urn update.

### Reading

The figure does three things together. It demonstrates that the basin of an ideal joint policy is non-empty and is reached from neutral initial conditions ($n = 1$) with positive probability — the bare existence claim the proof of concept needs. It demonstrates that the probability of reaching the basin grows monotonically in initial coordination and in the number of episodes the chain is allowed to run, placing a *quantitative* number on the reach time: thousands of episodes, dependent on initialization and on the additive Roth–Erev update. And it demonstrates that *informative* and *useful* signaling come apart in finite time, as a transient signature visible at intermediate horizons.

This is the *honest* version of the §2.3 claim. The old framing made the same point with a corner case: at `init_weights = (1, 0)`, the deterministic-init Roth–Erev chain is frozen — NMI sits at $1.0$ because each signal is a deterministic function of the observation, but reward sits at the random-action baseline $0.25$ because the chain is stuck at a uniformly random one of the $2304$ deterministic joint policies, only $4$ of which are ideal. That observation is correct but cartoonish: it depends on a non-generic initialization that real learners would never start from. The continuous sweep shown above carries the same lesson — high NMI is not sufficient for high reward — in a non-degenerate empirical regime that real systems land in. The lesson is, in fact, *stronger* here: the dissociation appears not only at the cartoon endpoint but along the whole low-coordination edge of the sweep, and unfolds along an explicit time dimension that the cartoon endpoint could not display.

### What this is, and what this is not

Three things should be kept separate at this point. *What this is*: a demonstration that, in a Markov chain on policy space whose only fixed points are deterministic policies, the basin of an ideal policy is non-empty and is reached from a wide range of initial conditions in finite time. The reach probability depends quantitatively and smoothly on initialization; and the mechanism that produces communication when it does emerge is a genuine attractor, with rewards near an ideal profile reinforcing being even nearer. *What this is not*: a proof of convergence in probability. A theorem of that kind exists for the symmetric, cooperative-payoff Lewis–Skyrms signaling game under Roth–Erev (Argiento et al. 2009), but it relies on a property that fails in our setting: in the cooperative case the urn dynamics is the gradient flow of a single shared expected-reward potential, and convergence to its stable critical points then follows from Pemantle's stable-manifold theorem. With distributed rewards each agent gradient-follows its own expected reward; no single potential exists; and the cooperative argument does not lift directly. (The precise step that breaks down — and the routes that might recover a global convergence statement — are documented in [analytics/math/argiento_obstruction.md](argiento_obstruction.md).) Whether some other route to a global convergence statement is available is genuinely open. *What follows methodologically*: the proof of concept gives existence and a *quantitative* dependence on initialization. The figure puts numbers on how reliable the basin reach is for Roth–Erev in this game: at horizon $H = 10{,}000$, mean final reward across $500$ seeds sits well above the no-signaling baseline of $0.5$ across the entire bias sweep, rising from $\approx 0.65$ at the unbiased start ($n = 1$) to $\approx 0.99$ under strong initial bias ($n = 100$). What it does not give is *general* reliability across learning rules and game structures; that is a separate quantitative question, best answered by simulations of the sort §3 reports.
