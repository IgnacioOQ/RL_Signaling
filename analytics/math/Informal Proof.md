# An informal proof of concept — the canonical signaling game

This note is the §2.3 sketch, restricted to the smallest non-trivial setup: two agents, two binary features, two signals, four actions. Everything stated here has a rigorous companion under [analytics/](../) — those are cited inline. The aim is to walk the argument carefully enough that the formal companions are clearly *what* gets proved, not *whether* it can be proved.

## 1. Setup: the canonical signaling game

Two agents, $i \in \{0, 1\}$. Each episode:

1. **Nature** draws a binary world state $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2) \in \{0, 1\}^2$ uniformly at random.
2. **Observation.** Agent $0$ sees only $v_1$; agent $1$ sees only $v_2$.
3. **Signal.** Each agent emits a signal $\sigma_i \in \{0, 1\}$, sampled from its *signaling policy* $f^{(i)}$ — a $2 \times 2$ table with one row per observation and two propensities per row.
4. **Action.** Each agent receives the signal sent by the other and picks a final action $\alpha_i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$, sampled from its *action policy* $g^{(i)}$ — a $4 \times 4$ table keyed by (own observation, received signal).
5. **Reward.** Agent $i$ collects $r_i = G_i(\mathbf{v}, \alpha_i) \in \{0, 1\}$ from its own *matching game* $G_i$. A canonical matching game is one where, for each world state $\mathbf{v}$, exactly one action $\alpha^\star_i(\mathbf{v}) \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ pays $1$ and the other three pay $0$.

The crucial feature is that agent $i$'s reward depends on the *full* world state $\mathbf{v}$, not just on the part it observes. So reaching the optimum requires $i$ to learn from $j$'s signal — and vice versa — even though the two payoffs $G_0$ and $G_1$ are independent.

### Ideal strategies

Call a joint policy $(f^{(0)}, g^{(0)}, f^{(1)}, g^{(1)})$ **ideal** if both agents earn reward 1 on every world state. Two things are needed simultaneously:

- $f^{(j)}$ must be a *bijection* observation → signal, so the signal carries the partner's observation losslessly.
- $g^{(i)}$ must be the unique action map satisfying $g^{(i)}\bigl(\mathbf{o}_i,\, f^{(j)}(\mathbf{o}_j)\bigr) = \alpha^\star_i(\mathbf{v})$ for every $\mathbf{v}$.

In the canonical case there are $2$ signaling bijections per agent, and given the partner's bijection a *unique* correct action map per agent. So there are exactly $2 \times 2 = 4$ ideal joint profiles. (The full enumeration is in [analytics/math/proof_of_concept_markov.md](proof_of_concept_markov.md), §"Counting absorbing states".)

The hypothesis we want to defend is: *despite each agent's payoff being independent of the other's action, learning carries the joint policy into one of these 4 ideal profiles, with non-trivial probability.*

## 2. The Markov chain on policy space

Fix the learning rule (Roth–Erev) and the games $G_0$, $G_1$. Then everything stochastic in an episode — the world state, the signals, the actions, the rewards — is generated by the policies in play *at the start of the episode*. So the right state of the system, in the Markov-chain sense, is just the policies:

$$\sigma_t \;=\; \bigl( f^{(0)}_t,\; g^{(0)}_t,\; f^{(1)}_t,\; g^{(1)}_t \bigr).$$

The per-episode draws ($\mathbf{v}$, $\sigma_0$, $\sigma_1$, $\alpha_0$, $\alpha_1$, $r_0$, $r_1$) are *outputs* of the transition, not part of the state — including them would double-count the randomness. They are the random ingredients that drive $\sigma_t \mapsto \sigma_{t+1}$ and are then forgotten.

The transition is a five-step composition:

1. Nature draws $\mathbf{v}$ uniformly.
2. Each agent samples a signal from its own row $f^{(i)}_t[\mathbf{o}_i]$, with probability proportional to the urn weights.
3. Each agent receives the partner's signal and samples an action from $g^{(i)}_t[(\mathbf{o}_i, \sigma_{j(i)})]$, again proportional to the weights.
4. Rewards $r_0, r_1 \in \{0, 1\}$ are read off from $G_0, G_1$.
5. The chosen cells get incremented by their respective rewards (Roth–Erev update).

Because the update only ever *adds* non-negative reinforcement to the cell that was just used, urn cells with weight $0$ stay at $0$ forever. This will matter in §4.

> **A useful first observation.** A policy $\sigma$ is an *absorbing state* of the chain exactly when every cell of every urn is *one-hot*: a single positive entry, the rest zero. At a one-hot policy every signal and every action is deterministic given its key, so the only cell ever touched by an episode is the cell whose unique positive entry is the one being sampled — and incrementing that entry leaves the cell still one-hot. Conversely, any cell with two or more positive entries can have its smaller entry incremented with positive probability, which changes the policy. A counting argument (see [analytics/math/proof_of_concept_markov.md](proof_of_concept_markov.md) §"Counting absorbing states") shows there are $2304$ such one-hot joint policies — and only $4$ of them are ideal.

## 3. What "attractor" really means

The original informal argument said: the ideal strategies are an attractor, because the closer the system is to one, the more likely it gets even closer at the next step. There are two distinct claims hidden here, worth pulling apart:

- **(i) Basin reachability.** From a generic starting point, does the chain ever enter the basin of attraction of *some* ideal $\sigma^\star$?
- **(ii) Basin → ideal convergence.** Conditional on having entered such a basin, does the chain in fact converge to $\sigma^\star$?

The "closer ⇒ likelier to get closer" intuition is a claim about (ii): *given* we are already near an ideal profile, the rewards we collect tend to reinforce the cells that bring us closer. That intuition is right as far as it goes — but it is silent about (i), which is the harder and more important question.

This split also clarifies what the "miracle drift" picture is really claiming. The "miracle" is exactly (i): random initialization plus a finite number of lucky episodes carries the joint policy into the basin of an ideal $\sigma^\star$. Convergence inside the basin is a comparatively mild claim; entering the basin in the first place is the substantive event.

## 4. The per-cell sub-martingale argument, and where it stops

Here is the cleanest formal version of (ii). Take the simplest possible reduction: one agent, one observation, two signals, and a fixed *correct* signal $\sigma^\star$ that pays reward $1$ (and the other signal pays $0$). With initial weights $(n, m)$, write the urn as $(u_{\text{hot}, t}, u_{\text{cold}})$, where the hot cell tracks the correct signal. The wrong-signal cell is never reinforced, so $u_{\text{cold}} = m$ for all time. The hot-cell *fraction*

$$\rho_t \;=\; \frac{u_{\text{hot}, t}}{u_{\text{hot}, t} + m}$$

is a sub-martingale: at each step, with probability $\rho_t$ the hot cell grows by 1 and $\rho_t$ ticks up; otherwise it stays put. By martingale convergence plus a non-trivial-increment lower bound, $\rho_t \to 1$ almost surely **provided $m > 0$**. The closed-form recursions and matching simulations are in [analytics/scripts/study_toy_markov_chain.py](../scripts/study_toy_markov_chain.py).

This is the cleanest rigorous statement of "the agent learns the right answer." It captures the §2.3 attractor intuition exactly, in the simplest setting where the intuition actually holds.

The next-largest tractable case lifts this to a real signaling row, by freezing the *partner* but letting agent $i$'s own row run free. Concretely, fix the partner $j$'s entire policy $(f^{(j)}, g^{(j)})$ and agent $i$'s own action policy $g^{(i)}$ for all time. Then for each observation $x$, the row $f^{(i)}_t[x]$ evolves as a *Bernoulli-thinned Pólya urn*: every visit to that row reinforces the sampled signal cell with some *constant per-color probability* $q^\star(x)$. The constancy across colors is the non-obvious fact — and it turns the row into an honest Pólya urn (see [analytics/math/proof_of_concept_markov.md](proof_of_concept_markov.md) §"Pure-Pólya signaling-urn convergence" for the proof). The classical Eggenberger–Pólya theorem then gives:

> Conditional on $q^\star(x) > 0$, the proportion vector $\hat{f}^{(i)}_t[x]$ converges almost surely to a *random* limit on the simplex with law $\mathrm{Dir}(n_0)$, where $n_0 = f^{(i)}_0[x]$ is the row's initial weight vector.

This is strictly stronger than the toy single-state model — it lets the row have any number of signals, not just two, and it does not require the reward to be "all-or-nothing concentrated on the right signal." But it gives back something *weaker* than what §2.3 wants: the row concentrates *somewhere*, but the limit is a function of the initial weights and the realized noise — not of which signaling code happens to be correct given $G_i$.

So the per-cell sub-martingale argument does real work, but it stops short of what §2.3 needs in three ways.

1. **It is per-cell, not joint.** Both reductions freeze the partner. In the actual joint chain the partner is also drifting, so the "right answer" each cell is concentrating toward is a moving target.

2. **The Pólya limit is random, not ideal.** Even with the partner frozen, $\hat{f}^{(i)}_t[x]$ lands at *some* simplex point, not necessarily one that is correct given $G_i$. Concentration is not selection.

3. **At $m = 0$ the argument fails outright** — and the failure is visible empirically. The setting `init_weights = (1, 0)` puts every urn cell into a one-hot configuration at $t = 0$, which is by §2 already an absorbing state. The chain *never moves*. The realized joint policy is whichever of the $2304$ absorbing states the random initialization picked, and only $4 / 2304 \approx 0.17\%$ of those are ideal. Concretely, this is the **`(1,0)` paradox**: NMI saturates at $1.0$ (because the policy is deterministic — signals carry their observations losslessly), while the mean reward sits at exactly $0.25 = 1/M$, the random-action baseline. The chain *converges* — trivially, by sitting still — but to a uniformly-random absorbing state, almost certainly not to any of the four ideal ones. (See [analytics/math/initialization_basins.md](initialization_basins.md) §"The (1, 0) case" for the full breakdown.)

Reading these three together: the per-cell argument is the *easy half* of the story. It establishes (ii) cleanly and rules out a few naive failure modes. The *hard half* — which is genuinely open — is what §5 takes up.

## 5. The honest open problem

What remains open is question (i): **basin reachability for the joint chain**. The structural picture is:

- The chain has $2304$ absorbing states. Only $4$ are ideal; $324$ are total traps where both agents collect reward $0$ on every world state. The mean reward over the full absorbing set is exactly $1/M = 0.25$ — the random-action baseline. Equilibrium statistics alone do not tell us the joint chain's law concentrates on the ideal subset rather than on the trap subset.
- For $m > 0$ the chain has no absorbing states, but it has the same set of asymptotic concentration points. The substantive question is the *measure* the chain's law puts on each of them — in particular, on the four ideal ones — as $t \to \infty$.

There is one published convergence result in this neighborhood: Argiento, Pemantle, Skyrms, and Volkov (2009) prove almost-sure convergence to the separating signaling equilibria for the *cooperative-payoff* Lewis–Skyrms game (where both agents share a single payoff function). Their argument has three layers:

1. Embed the discrete urn dynamics as a continuous-time stochastic-approximation process $\dot\theta = h(\theta) + \text{noise}$ on the simplex.
2. *Identify the mean field $h$ as the gradient of a single shared expected-reward potential* $W(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[G(\mathbf{v}, \alpha)]$.
3. Apply Pemantle's stable-manifold theorem, which delivers almost-sure convergence to the linearly stable critical points of $W$ from a neighborhood of each.

Step 2 is the load-bearing one — the existence of $W$ is what lets step 3 go through, and what rules out limit cycles and other non-pointwise recurrent sets that could otherwise capture the SA process. Step 2 also fails for our setting. With distributed rewards, each agent gradient-follows its *own* expected reward $W_i(\theta)$, and a global potential $W$ with $\partial W / \partial \theta_i = \nabla_{\theta_i} W_i$ exists only if the cross-Hessians satisfy a symmetry condition that fails for generic distributed-reward games. Without a global $W$, Pemantle's stable-manifold theorem does not apply directly, and the joint flow could in principle have non-pointwise recurrent sets — limit cycles, heteroclinic chains — that the cooperative argument rules out for free. Three concrete salvage routes — local linear stability at each ideal $\sigma^\star$, a sum-potential check on $W^\Sigma = \sum_i W_i$, and the differential-inclusion framework of Benaïm–Hofbauer–Sorin (2005) — are documented in [analytics/math/argiento_obstruction.md](argiento_obstruction.md).

So the honest position of this proof of concept, as of the present draft, is:

- **Per-cell convergence with the partner frozen** is rigorously established (Pure-Pólya theorem, $m > 0$).
- **Joint almost-sure convergence to the ideal set** is open. The only published machinery for the analogous problem (Argiento et al. 2009) needs a global Lyapunov potential, and that potential does not exist in our distributed-reward setting.
- **Empirically**, simulations from a uniform-or-stronger start ($m > 0$) reach high reward and high NMI in the canonical case across a range of `init_weights`. The formal explanation for *why*, and for the boundary of the parameter regime in which it does, is the remaining work.

The per-cell argument is the easy half. The Argiento obstruction is the gate to the hard half. Neither half is the whole proof of concept; together they say what is rigorously true today and what is left to do.
